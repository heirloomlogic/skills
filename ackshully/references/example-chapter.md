# Lear gives away the map and keeps the crown

Actually factually, Lear does not retire in Act 1. He gives away the land, the revenue and the administration, and keeps the title, the ceremony and a hundred knights — which is the arrangement the rest of the play spends five acts dismantling. By the end of the act he has lost fifty of the knights and started to notice.

*King Lear*, Shakespeare, c. 1605–06; Quarto 1608, Folio 1623. Act 1, five scenes.

## The longer version

The act sets both of its plots running the same way: an old father asks a child to prove love in words, believes the child who lies, and disinherits the one who will not.

It opens mid-conversation between Kent and Gloucester about who inherits, and Gloucester makes a joke about having fathered Edmund out of wedlock while Edmund stands there. Then Lear enters with a map. He proposes to divide Britain into three and to award the portions by a love test. Goneril and Regan compete in superlatives. Cordelia, his favourite and the one he has already planned to give the largest third, answers "Nothing." Lear disinherits her on the spot, splits her share between her sisters, and banishes Kent for objecting. The Duke of Burgundy withdraws his suit once the dowry is gone; the King of France takes her without one and leaves with her.

The second scene starts the parallel. Edmund, Gloucester's younger and illegitimate son, forges a letter in his brother Edgar's hand proposing that they kill their father for his estate, and lets Gloucester find it. Gloucester believes it immediately. Edgar goes into hiding by the end of the scene.

The last three scenes are the arrangement failing. Goneril, whose house Lear is living in, instructs her steward Oswald to be insolent to him. Kent returns in disguise and takes service with Lear under a false name. The Fool arrives and spends the act telling Lear, in riddles that Lear does not stop, that he has given away the thing that made him a king. Goneril demands he cut his hundred knights to fifty. Lear curses her with sterility and leaves for Regan's, still expecting to find the other daughter different.

## Who is who

| Person | In Act 1 | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Lear | Divides the kingdom, disowns Cordelia, banishes Kent, quarrels with Goneril | He asks "Who is it that can tell me who I am?" in 1.4 and means it as a rebuke |
| Goneril, Regan | Win the test with set speeches, then compare notes on his "poor judgement" in private at the end of 1.1 | They agree only while they have a common target |
| Cordelia | Refuses the test, is disinherited, leaves for France | She is absent from 1.2 to the end of Act 3 |
| Kent | Objects, is banished, returns disguised and serves anyway | The only character who tells Lear the truth and survives Act 1 |
| Gloucester | Jokes about Edmund's birth, believes the forged letter | His plot runs one step behind Lear's for the whole play |
| Edmund | Forges the letter, drives Edgar into hiding | He explains his plan to the audience before he executes it, every time |
| Edgar | Named in a letter he did not write; flees | He has almost no lines in Act 1 |
| The Fool | Enters in 1.4 | He says what Kent was banished for saying, and is not punished |

## The love test turns on one word

Lear asks the question as an auction — "Which of you shall we say doth love us most, / That we our largest bounty may extend / Where nature doth with merit challenge?" — and Cordelia answers by refusing the terms rather than the affection:

```text
I love your majesty
According to my bond, no more nor less.
```

Lear's reply, "Nothing will come of nothing. Speak again," is the act's hinge. He has already drawn the map and already chosen her third; the test was meant to be a formality that produced a public performance. Her refusal converts it into a real trial, and he loses it in front of the court, which is why the punishment is so far out of proportion.

## The Gloucester plot is the same plot

Edmund states his programme in a soliloquy at the top of 1.2, before he does anything:

```text
Thou, Nature, art my goddess; to thy law
My services are bound.
```

The scene then runs the Act 1 structure again at speed: a father, a document that stands in for a child's love, a credulous reading, and a disinheritance. Gloucester reaches for the eclipses to explain it. Edmund waits for him to leave, then tells the audience that blaming the stars is how men avoid blaming themselves — the closest thing in the play to a statement of its own method.

## The words that come back

- **Nothing.** Cordelia's answer, Lear's reply, and the Fool's arithmetic in 1.4 ("Can you make no use of nothing, nuncle?"). It returns in the storm.
- **Eyes and seeing.** Kent's "See better, Lear" in 1.1 is a metaphor. Gloucester's blinding in Act 3 is not.
- **Nature and natural.** Lear invokes nature to curse Goneril's womb; Edmund invokes it to justify himself. The two men mean opposite things by it.
- **Madness.** Lear names it himself at the end of 1.5 — "O, let me not be mad, not mad, sweet heaven!" — three acts before it arrives.

## What the text does not settle

- The act and scene divisions are editorial. The 1608 Quarto is printed without them.
- The Quarto and the 1623 Folio are substantively different texts. Modern editions conflate, choose, or print both, so line numbers do not agree across editions. Cite by scene.
